# Cortisol vs adrenaline — why the stress response is split across two hormones

**Question.** What is the evolutionary purpose of cortisol, and why is cortisol metabolically separated from adrenaline?

## Summary

The vertebrate stress response is split across two axes — a fast neural/amine alarm (SAM) and a slow hormonal/steroid mobilizer (HPA) — because "sprint" and "siege" are physically incompatible tasks for a single molecule. Adrenaline handles seconds-to-minutes mobilization; cortisol handles hours-to-weeks reallocation, permits adrenaline's vascular effects, brakes the acute response before it becomes self-destructive, and doubles as a circadian metabolic pacemaker.

## Two axes, two timescales, two chemistries

| | SAM axis | HPA axis |
|---|---|---|
| Signal | Adrenaline / noradrenaline (catecholamines) | Cortisol (glucocorticoid steroid) |
| Receptor class | Cell-surface GPCRs | Intracellular nuclear receptor (GR) |
| Mechanism | Second-messenger cascades | Gene transcription |
| Onset | Seconds | ~15–30 minutes |
| Duration | Minutes | Hours |
| Ancestry | Conserved across bilaterians | GR lineage traces to early jawed vertebrates (~450 Mya; MR/GR duplication) |

You cannot get both speed and durability out of one molecule. Fast signals exhaust their substrate pools and cannot rewrite cellular programs; slow signals cannot save you from a predator. Evolution built the two axes on different substrates precisely because the physics of the two jobs differ.

## Division of labor in one stress response

- **Adrenaline (immediate mobilization).** Cardiac output up, glycogen → glucose, bronchodilation, blood shunted to muscle, platelets primed, pupils dilated.
- **Cortisol (sustained reallocation).** Gluconeogenesis from amino acids, lipolysis, suppression of "expensive" long-horizon systems that do not help you survive the next hour — immune surveillance, reproduction, growth, digestion, bone deposition.
- **Permissive effect.** Cortisol *permits* catecholamine action on vasculature; without it, adrenaline's pressor effect collapses. The two axes are staged, not redundant.

## Why the separation is adaptive

- **Threat timescales differ.** Predator attacks resolve in seconds to minutes; wounds, infection, starvation, social subordination, and winter play out over hours to weeks. A SAM-only organism would outrun the leopard and die of the infected wound. A HPA-only organism would never outrun anything.
- **Negative feedback and braking.** Cortisol provides negative feedback onto the HPA axis and onto inflammation itself. A single-hormone system has no brake; the acute response would become self-destructive.
- **Second job: circadian pacing.** Cortisol peaks around waking and drives the morning glucose mobilization that gets a body moving each day. This second role would be impossible if the same molecule fired every time something startled you — another reason the slow axis uses a different chemistry from the fast one.

## Evolutionary sketch

The catecholamine alarm is the ancestral, conserved solution — present across bilaterians. The glucocorticoid receptor lineage traces to early jawed vertebrates; MR and GR diverged from a common ancestor via gene duplication (~450 Mya), after which GR specialized for glucocorticoid signaling. The slow steroid axis is layered on top of the older fast alarm, consistent with larger and longer-lived animals needing a mechanism to handle longer-duration threats than the original neural alarm could address.
